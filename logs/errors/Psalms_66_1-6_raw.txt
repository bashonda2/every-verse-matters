Paul in 1 Corinthians 10:1-4 writes: 'All our fathers were under the cloud, and all passed through the sea; And were all baptized unto Moses in the cloud and in the sea; And did all eat the same spiritual meat; And did all drink the same spiritual drink: for they drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them: and that Rock was Christ.' The Red Sea crossing prefigures Christian baptism (passing through water to new life); the manna and water in the wilderness prefigure the body and blood of Christ. The Jordan crossing represents entry into the promised land, which is a type of entering the celestial kingdom through Christ. The crossing on foot emphasizes that redemption is accomplished by God's power, not human effort, but that it requires human participation and faith. The psalmist's exultation ('there we rejoiced in him') anticipates the rejoicing of the redeemed in Christ's deliverance.",
      "application": "Psalm 66:6 invites modern believers to claim the mighty works of God as their own and to recognize that God's deliverance is continuous, not limited to ancient history. The shift from third person to first person ('we rejoiced') suggests several applications. First, it invites us to study and reflect on God's historical works—the Exodus, the conquest, the various deliverances recorded in scripture. These are not merely historical information but testimonies to God's character and power that apply to us. Second, it encourages us to recognize God's delivering power in our own lives. Just as the ancient Israelites were liberated from slavery and guided through the wilderness, modern believers experience liberation from sin and guidance through covenant. When we experience the power of the Atonement, the transforming work of the Spirit, or the provision of unexpected help in time of need, we are experiencing God's turning of water into dry land—the overturning of impossible circumstances. Third, the principle that God's ancient works become present reality through covenant community suggests that when we gather in worship, we participate in the rejoicing of all saints. When we sing hymns about God's works or bear testimony, we are joining a chorus that extends across time. Fourth, the verse invites us to become witnesses. Just as the psalmist invites others to 'come and see' and then declares 'we rejoiced,' we are invited to share our testimony of God's mighty works in our lives. Our personal experiences of God's deliverance become evidence that God continues to work mighty deeds. Fifth, in the context of Latter-day Saint faith, Psalm 66:6 reminds us that the Restoration itself is God's mighty work. Joseph Smith's visions, the restoration of priesthood, the coming forth of the Book of Mormon—these are 'works of God' comparable to the Exodus and the Jordan crossing. When we declare our faith in the Restoration, we are saying 'we rejoiced in him' as the God who continues to perform mighty deeds in our dispensation."
